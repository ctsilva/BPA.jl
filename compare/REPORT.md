# Cross-checking Ball-Pivoting implementations

*2026-09-02, second round.* Compares three implementations of the Ball-Pivoting Algorithm
(Bernardini et al., 1999) on the same inputs: **BPA.jl** (this package, at commit
`a5105c3`), **Open3D** 0.19 and **MeshLab**'s VCG filter (through PyMeshLab 2025). The harness is `compare.jl` next to this
file; see `README.md` for how to run it and how to add an implementation. Its outputs,
`results/report.md` with the full tables and `results/index.html` with the renderings side
by side, are regenerated by running it and are not kept in the repository.

A first round of this comparison, on 2026-09-02, found and fixed a pivot bug in BPA.jl
(the ball skipped the opposite vertex of the triangle it started from). The package then
changed its pivot normal test, reworked the seed search, the front and the pivot for speed,
and gained a component filter. This round reran everything on the new code with a larger
set of experiments and renderings, so that the holes, boundary loops and flipped patches
behind the numbers can be seen.

## 1. What the algorithm promises

The paper's ball pivoting builds a triangle mesh whose every triangle is a face of the
ρ-alpha-shape of the samples: a ball of radius ρ passes through its three vertices and
contains no other sample. Two things make this property hold in the paper's formulation:

- a **seed** triangle is accepted only after an explicit test that its ball is empty;
- a **pivot** rolls the ball around a front edge, keeping contact with the edge's endpoints,
  and stops at the *first* sample its surface reaches. Because the ball was empty at the
  start, and a point can only get inside by crossing the surface, the ball at the first
  contact is empty too. No further test is needed.

Everything else in the paper (join, glue, the front loops) is bookkeeping that keeps the
mesh orientable and manifold. The outcome is not unique: when several samples are touched
at the same angle (four cospherical points, as on any regular lattice) either choice is
valid, and the seed order decides which component grows first.

## 2. Conceptual differences between the implementations

All three share the outline: seeds, a front of edges, pivots. They differ in how faithfully
the pivot follows the paper and in what they do instead.

### BPA.jl

Follows the paper directly. The pivot orders, for every sample within 2ρ of the edge
midpoint, the position at which the moving ball's surface first reaches it, and returns
the first (since the performance work this is done without trigonometry, as a 2-D position
of the ball centre ordered by half-plane and cross product). Both ball positions through a
candidate, above and below the surface, are on the trajectory and both are considered. Ties
are resolved by a deterministic rule that prefers triangles gluing to existing front edges,
so cospherical quads never get conflicting diagonals. The front is a set of directed
half-edges in per-vertex chains, which makes a non-orientable or non-manifold result
impossible by construction. Seeds follow the paper's voxel heuristic, one candidate per
voxel, with the pair search bounded to the candidate's 100 nearest neighbours.

Two decisions taken since the first round matter for the comparison:

- **The opposite vertex is a candidate.** The ball leaves the third vertex of the triangle
  it starts from at once, but returns to it after about half a turn on a well-sampled
  surface; if that happens before any other point is touched, the pivot fails and the edge
  becomes a boundary edge. This is the fix from the first round (commit `5ae0f23`).
- **The pivot normal test uses only the landed-on point.** A pivot triangle is accepted if
  the normal of the point the ball lands on has a non-negative dot product with the
  triangle's normal. The edge already fixes the winding, so the test only has to catch the
  ball rolling onto the back of a nearby sheet. The first-round code tested all three vertex
  normals with a strict inequality, which refused every scan vertex without a normal (a
  vertex that belongs to no face of its scan) and the steep triangles joining overlapping
  scans, and left many more holes on merged scans.

### Open3D

`cpp/open3d/geometry/SurfaceReconstructionBallPivoting.cpp`. For each candidate it computes
only the *outward* ball through the edge and the candidate (oriented by the sum of the three
vertex normals), takes the angle to its centre, and then **scans the whole neighbourhood to
verify that this ball is empty**. The candidate returned is the smallest-angle point whose
outward ball is empty. This is not the paper's rolling ball, but it produces the same faces
wherever the answer is determined, because it enumerates the same alpha-shape faces and
picks by angle. Consequences:

- every emitted triangle has an empty outward ball, guaranteed by brute force;
- each pivot costs the square of the neighbourhood size, so it is slower as ρ grows;
- a candidate whose first contact is with the inner ball position is never seen at that
  angle;
- edges are undirected objects shared between triangles, so nothing enforces winding.
  Meshes come out non-orientable on the knot and on every scan case;
- seeds are tried at every vertex in index order, without the voxel heuristic;
- the normal test requires the face normal to agree with all three vertex normals, so a
  vertex without a normal can never be used.

The opposite vertex is skipped here as well; the emptiness scan is what catches the
resulting bad balls.

### MeshLab (VCG)

`vcg/complex/algorithms/create/ball_pivoting.h`, the code behind MeshLab's "Surface
Reconstruction: Ball Pivoting". It departs furthest from the paper:

- candidates are the **16 nearest samples** to the edge midpoint, not everything in reach;
- interior vertices are removed from the candidate list rather than stopping the pivot, so
  the ball can roll through them;
- **clustering**: every sample within 0.2ρ of a vertex that enters the mesh is marked used
  and dropped. Deliberate decimation against slivers, and a trap: whenever 0.2ρ reaches the
  sample spacing the filter silently thins the input (the regular torus at ρ = 0.10 keeps
  every other vertex; the area-sampled torus keeps three quarters);
- pivots beyond about 177° are refused (the "half-turn cap"), which is what stands in for
  handling the opposite vertex. It is wrong in both directions: legitimate hits between the
  cap and the true return angle are lost, and when the ball starts leaning away from the
  opposite vertex the return comes before the cap and is not protected;
- the seed emptiness test tolerates points 0.2ρ inside the ball;
- seed orientation ignores normals altogether: a seed is accepted if its normal points away
  from the barycentre of the whole cloud. Wrong on any concave region;
- the compatibility test is a 90° limit between consecutive face normals, not a test
  against vertex normals; face windings come out reversed relative to the normals on
  whole components;
- detecting whether a candidate is on the front walks every front edge.

## 3. Methodology

`compare.jl` runs all three tools on each case and evaluates every output the same way. The
other tools receive the merged cloud as a NOFF file written by BPA.jl, so vertex indices
are identical across all outputs (verified: same count, same positions). MeshLab is given
the radius as an absolute value, not a percentage of the bounding box.

**Per-output checks**, independent of any reference:

1. *Empty-ball test.* For each triangle, take the ball of radius ρ through its vertices on
   its outward side and query the voxel grid for other samples inside it. The outward side
   is the one all three vertex normals agree on; when they do not (a vertex without a
   normal, or a normal at right angles to a steep triangle between two scan layers) both
   sides are tried, the winding side first, so every triangle gets the ball test and passes
   if either ball is empty. Classes: `valid`; `valid_reversed_winding` (the triangle is
   wound against the outward side but its outward ball is empty); `ball_not_empty`, with
   the depth of the deepest intruder as a fraction of ρ; `ball_not_empty_tie` for depth
   below 1e-5 ρ, which is a cospherical tie and not an error; `circumradius_too_large`;
   `degenerate`.
2. *Topology*, using BPA.jl's `check_mesh`: orientable (no directed half-edge used
   twice), edge-manifold (at most two triangles per edge), vertex-manifold, boundary edges
   and loops, connected components and the size of the largest, Euler characteristic. On
   the closed synthetic surfaces the exact answer is known (sphere χ = 2, torus χ = 0, two
   triangles per vertex).
3. *Renderings*, with `tools/render.jl`, saved as PNG in `results/<case>/render/`:
   the mesh flat-shaded with its boundary edges in red; the number of triangles behind each
   pixel, warm colours for odd counts and cool for even, so that for a closed surface every
   warm pixel is a hole seen through (including holes on the far side); and front-facing
   minus back-facing triangles per pixel, grey where they cancel, blue or red at holes and
   flipped patches. For scan lists the input scans are rendered too, with the number of
   overlapping scans per pixel. The share of odd-parity pixels and of non-cancelling pixels
   goes into the tables as a view-dependent but reference-free measure of holes.

**Comparison with BPA.jl.** Triangle sets are compared as unordered vertex triples: common,
only in BPA.jl, only in the other, and how many of the common ones have the same winding.
For each triangle present on one side only, the number of its edges that the other mesh
also has is recorded: 2 or 3 means the same region triangulated differently, 0 or 1 means
one front advanced where the other stopped.

**Diagonal switching** (`flip.jl`). To separate free choices from real disagreement, the
other tool's mesh is post-processed toward BPA.jl's: any two adjacent triangles forming a
quad whose other diagonal BPA.jl uses are replaced by BPA.jl's pair, repeated to a fixed
point; then any connected patch of differing triangles is swapped for the BPA.jl patch with
exactly the same boundary. Whatever survives is a triangle one tool built and the other did
not; `remaining.jl` classifies it.

**Cases.** Synthetic surfaces with a known answer, and Stanford range scans. The synthetic
inputs are generated into `results/inputs/`; the scans come from `data/` of the package,
each scan's vertex normals derived from its own triangles and moved by its `.xf` matrix.

median nearest-neighbour distance (0.0102) is almost exactly 0.2ρ, it drops a quarter of
the points and leaves 94 boundary loops and 11338 triangles with a non-empty ball. On the
jittered torus (spacing 0.029, above 0.2ρ = 0.012) it does fine but still loses four
triangles.

## 5. Conclusions

- **BPA.jl and Open3D compute the same alpha-shape faces.** Wherever the answer is
  determined they agree exactly (jittered and sampled torus: identical output; regular
  torus: identical after resolving ties). Open3D verifies emptiness by brute force, BPA.jl
  by the paper's first-hit argument, and on every case, synthetic or scanned, neither
  produces a single triangle with a non-empty ball. The differences on scans come from
  the normal test (BPA.jl's is deliberately looser, and fills more of the seams between
  scans), from Open3D's missing edge orientation (its meshes are non-orientable on every
  scan case and on the knot) and from seeding order. BPA.jl is about 3 times faster on the
  small inputs, 25 times on the ten-scan bunny and 130 times on the dragon; the gap widens
  with the number of points per ball because Open3D's pivot is quadratic in the
  neighbourhood size.
- **MeshLab's filter is a different algorithm** in practice: nearest-16 candidates, a
  half-turn cap, decimation, barycentre-based seed orientation. It produces a mesh, but on
  merged scans most of its triangles violate the empty-ball property, and its clustering
  threshold makes results depend on ρ relative to the sampling in a way the paper's
  algorithm does not. The area-sampled torus is the cleanest demonstration: a closed
  surface that the other two reconstruct perfectly, which MeshLab returns with a quarter
  of the points removed and 94 holes. On the dragon it needs four hours to triangulate the
  overlap layers separately.
- **The relaxed pivot normal test is a net gain.** It costs nothing on the synthetic cases
  (identical output), keeps every ball empty, and closes a fifth of the holes on the merged
  scans. The triangles it admits are exactly the ones Open3D cannot build.
- **What to rerun after touching the pivot, the front or the seed search:** `compare.jl
  synthetic` (the sphere, plane, jittered torus and sampled torus must stay identical
  between BPA.jl and Open3D, and non-empty balls must stay at zero everywhere), then
  `compare.jl scans` and `flip.jl open3d` to see whether any new difference with Open3D is
  a free choice or real.

## 6. Caveats

- The render-based hole measures depend on the view (30° about the vertical axis, 15°
  elevation, 40° for the tori) and, for open inputs such as the four-scan bunny, include
  the unscanned regions and, on the dragon, the turntable fragments; they are comparable
  between tools on the same case, not between cases.
- MeshLab's clustering (0.2ρ) must be kept below the sample spacing for its output to be
  comparable; the regular torus at ρ = 0.10 and the sampled torus at ρ = 0.05 show what
  happens otherwise.
- The empty-ball test uses the outward ball, or either ball when the normals disagree. A
  triangle reached through the inner ball position with agreeing normals would be flagged
  even if the paper's algorithm produced it; none occurred.
- Timings are single runs; Open3D's and MeshLab's include Python binding overhead and
  BPA.jl's may include residual JIT compilation.
- All source-code observations about Open3D and VCG are from reading the files on
  2026-09-02 (Open3D `main`, vcglib `main`); behaviour of other versions may differ.
